## Literature matrix
| Ref | What they did | Key findings | Limitations | How I use it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [1] Mackay et al. 2019, *NEJM* | Cohort of 7,676 former Scottish professionals vs matched controls, using death records | Neurodegenerative disease as cause of death 1.7% vs 0.5% (about 3.5×) | Association only; death-certificate data; historical players | Motivation (Introduction) |
| [2] Russell et al. 2021, *JAMA Neurology* | Same cohort, analysed by playing position and career length | Goalkeepers not at raised risk; defenders highest (HR ≈ 5); longer careers, higher risk; no change for players born 1910–1969 | Observational; heading exposure not measured | Links risk to heading; the "era" result matters for the vintage-ball extension |
| [3] Naunheim et al. 2003, *MSSE* | 4 adult players heading balls at 9 and 12 m/s; head-mounted accelerometers; compared with a simple model | ≈16 g at 9 m/s and ≈20 g at 12 m/s; HIC and GSI far below injury levels | Only 4 subjects; sensors on the head rather than the skull | Validation data for the model and the rig |
| [4] Di Virgilio et al. 2016, *EBioMedicine* | 19 amateur players headed machine-fired balls; brain function (TMS) and memory tested before and after | Raised corticomotor inhibition and poorer memory straight after heading; back to normal by 24 h | Small sample; short-term effects only | Shows that one session has measurable effects |
| [5] FA 2021, heading guidance | Guidance for adult football | Professional training: max 10 "higher-force" headers per week | Recommended, not enforced; limit not based on measured loads | Policy context |
| [6] FA 2024, youth heading rules | Rules after the IFAB trial | Deliberate heading phased out of U7–U11 matches by 2026/27 | – | Policy context; youth focus for recommendations |
| [7] Shewchenko et al. 2005, *BJSM* | Numerical human model plus 3 volunteers; 3 ball masses, 4 pressures | Halving pressure cut head/neck response by 10–31%; ball construction up to 15% | Only 3 volunteers; partly model-based | Supports a pressure effect |
| [8] Dezman et al. 2013, *Sports Health* | Collegiate players; neck flexor/extensor strength measured; low-speed hand-served headers | Strength imbalance correlated with angular head acceleration (ρ ≈ 0.50) | Small sample; low speed; correlation | Neck background |
| [9] Caccese et al. 2018, *Sports Biomechanics* | 100 players, 12 controlled headers each; accelerometer and gyroscope | Head/neck size explained ≈22% and neck strength ≈13–17% of the variation in head acceleration | Correlation; neck stiffness not isolated | Size of the neck effect |
| [10] Peek et al. 2020, *JSAMS* | Systematic review | Higher neck strength linked with lower head acceleration (cross-sectional studies) | Low-level evidence (cross-sectional) | Neck background |
| [11] Ueda et al. 2023, *Lancet Public Health* | 6,007 Swedish top-division players (1924–2019) vs 56,168 controls; health registers | 9% vs 6% diagnosed (≈1.5×); goalkeepers not significantly raised | Association; mostly mid-20th-century players | Recent confirmation; smaller risk than the Scottish study |
| [12] Barnes-Wood et al. 2024, *Ann. Biomed. Eng.* | 7 university players; custom-fit instrumented mouthguard; 10 lab headers from different pass distances; brain finite-element model | Peak linear 26 ± 7.9 g; peak angular 1730 ± 611 rad/s² | Small sample; laboratory headers | Recent skull-coupled values; rotational data |
| [13] Sokol-Randell et al. 2023, *Applied Sciences* | Mouthguards on 10 male collegiate players over 12 matches; 133 video-checked headers | Passes most common; goal kicks gave the highest angular acceleration; ≈27% of headers below 10 g | Pilot; one team of collegiate men | Real match conditions; informs the speed range |
| [14] Rowson & Duma 2013, *Ann. Biomed. Eng.* | Risk model from 63,011 American-football impacts (37 concussions) | Concussion risk depends on linear *and* rotational acceleration | American-football data | Justifies adding rotation later |
| [15] IFAB 2025, Law 2 | Rule | 68–70 cm, 410–450 g, 0.6–1.1 atm at sea level | – | Sets the test pressure range |
| [16] Queen et al. 2003, *MSSE* | Theoretical contact (Hertz) model of heading in children; rigid vs free neck | Pressure had little effect; heavier head, lower acceleration; rigid neck lowers acceleration and HIC | Theory only; children | Only study combining ball properties and neck stiffness, so it defines the gap |
| [17] Cecchi et al. 2020, *Sports Engineering* | Anthropomorphic test head and neck; ball launcher; 5 pressures (34–76 kPa) at 17.3, 19.7 and 22.2 m/s | Lower pressure lowered peak linear and rotational acceleration; higher speed raised both | One fixed neck; a surrogate, not players | Strongest support for a pressure effect; model for the rig method |
| [18] England et al. 2025, *Ann. Biomed. Eng.* | Pneumatic ball cannon at ≈17 m/s; 0.6 vs 1.0 bar; 34 combinations of impact location, head orientation, neck flexion and ball type | Lower pressure reduced or did not change head kinematics in most cases; "not a universal solution" | One speed; neck stiffness not varied | Newest evidence; orientation and location matter |
| [19] Austin et al. 2022, *ISBS Proceedings* | 12 male players; 0.4 and 0.54 atm; 5 and 8 m/s; motion capture | Lower pressure reduced impact force but **not** head acceleration | Conference paper; narrow range; low speeds | Conflicting human result, central to the gap |
| [20] Becker et al. 2019, *J. Sports Sci. Med.* | 33 male players; 6 weeks of neck flexor/extensor training; pendulum headers | No clear reduction in head acceleration | Short programme; small groups | Training doesn't guarantee lower acceleration, so the mechanism is open |
| [21] Peek et al. 2022, *Sports Medicine* | Pilot trial with 52 adolescents; FIFA 11+ with neck exercises, 3× a week for 5 weeks | Neck strength +53.8% (controls +15.6%); peak linear head acceleration −11.8% (controls −5.0%) | Pilot; small; heading technique may confound | Neck training *can* help, so test coupling directly on the rig |
| [22] Analog Devices, ADXL375 data sheet | Sensor data sheet | ±200 g range; output rate up to 3200 Hz | – | Sensor choice |

## Synthesis map: where the evidence agrees and disagrees
| Theme | Most studies agree that… | But… | Gap my project fills |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brain health | Former professionals are at higher risk, and goalkeepers are not [1, 2, 11] | The size of the risk varies (≈1.5× to 3.5×), and the studies can't say which impacts matter | Find which impact features can be reduced |
| Severity of one header | Single headers are far below concussion thresholds [3, 12] | Values depend on speed, delivery and sensor [12, 13] | Controlled, repeatable measurement |
| Ball pressure | Lower pressure lowers impact force [19] and head acceleration on surrogates [7, 17, 18] | No effect on players [19]; theory predicts little effect [16] | Test pressure while varying neck coupling |
| The neck | Stronger necks go with lower acceleration [8–10] | Training results are mixed [20, 21]; the mechanism is untested | Vary neck stiffness directly on the rig |
| Test methods | Surrogates are repeatable; mouthguards give field data [12, 13, 17] | Surrogates may not behave like players | A low-cost validated headform [22] |
