# How to write a good preliminary report: a step-by-step guide (ECM3175)

This guide walks you through every stage of producing the preliminary report **yourself**: what to do, why, what a good result looks like, and how to check you're done. It doesn't write the report for you. Where it shows examples, they use a *different* topic (running-shoe cushioning), so you learn the technique and then apply it to your own project.

Work through the stages in order. Tick the "Done when" list at the end of each stage before you move on. Altogether this takes roughly **30–35 hours** over three weeks.

## Stage 1: Understand the task

**Goal:** know exactly what the report must contain and how it will be judged, before you write a word.

### The rules
- **Length:** maximum **5 pages**. The example reports you were given run to page 5 with the cover as page 1, so count the cover as one of your 5 pages. Ask your supervisor if you're unsure.
- **Format:** use the module template (`Template-Preliminary_report.doc`), which sets the cover page, headings and layout.
- **Submission:** a **PDF on ELE** by **12:00 noon on Fri 4 December 2026**. The upload limit is 15 MB. If something goes wrong, contact Harrison student services, not your supervisor.
- **Plagiarism:** the module slides warn about penalties. Every sentence must be in your own words, and every idea from a source needs a reference.

### What the four sections must contain (from the module slide "Preliminary Report Contents")
| Section | Must include |
|---|---|
| **Introduction** | Introduce the project: what you will do and why, with **aims and objectives** |
| **Background and literature review** | What has been done before, showing that you are making a **novel contribution**; correct referencing |
| **Preliminary work** | Any work you've already done to prepare for the main project |
| **Project plan** | Methodologies, resources, deliverables, a **Gantt chart**, expected activities, **risk assessment**, **sustainability** and **health & safety**, and how you'll **manage** the project |

### Why it matters beyond this deadline
The preliminary report is the first draft of your final report:
- your literature review becomes Chapter 2 of the final report
- your plan becomes the "planned" Gantt chart you compare against in Chapter 7

The final report is marked on things like a *critical* literature review, *precise* aim and objectives, and *clear evidence of progress*. Writing to that standard now saves you work later.

### Learn from the two examples you were given
Read both again with a pen. This is what I noticed:

| | Example 1 (inventory management) | Example 2 (house energy model) |
|---|---|---|
| **Good** | A clear aim with three numbered objectives. Real preliminary work: a site visit and an interview. A proper Gantt chart. Named project risks with likelihoods | A **critical** literature review: it points out what each source *didn't* do (e.g. "failed to consider other potential algorithms"). Real preliminary data: a survey, an appliance-use chart and house measurements. A clear block diagram of the method |
| **Weak** | The literature review is mostly background statistics rather than evaluation. A typo in an objective ("analyse date"). Reference [9] is used twice. No health & safety or sustainability | No explicit aim and objectives list. A one-line risk section ("no major concerns for risk"). No health & safety or sustainability. Inconsistent reference formatting |

**Lesson:** take Example 2's critical style and Example 1's clear aim, objectives and risk table, and fix the gaps both of them left.

## Stage 2: Plan your time

**Goal:** a realistic schedule so nothing is rushed.

1. **Work backwards from the deadline.** Submit on **Thu 3 Dec**, one day early. Uploads fail, and the deadline is 12:00, not midnight.
2. **Block fixed sessions in your calendar.** For example, three 2–3-hour sessions a week, treated like lectures.
3. **Do the time-sensitive things first.** Your supervisor meeting and the ball-drop experiment depend on other people and equipment, so book them now.
4. **Leave 2 days of slack** before submission, for illness or other coursework.
5. **Start your logbook today.** Date every entry: meetings, decisions, sources read, test results. It's evidence of your project management, and it helps in the May interview.

**Done when:**
- [ ] Your study sessions are in your calendar up to 3 Dec.
- [ ] Your logbook exists, and its first entry is dated.

## Stage 3: Pin down the project (title, aim, objectives)

**Goal:** a clear, agreed scope. Everything else depends on this.

### Step 3.1: Meet your supervisor
Email them to ask for a meeting this week. Take a one-page summary of your idea and these questions:
- Is this scope right for the "Sport Injury Biomechanics" title?
- What equipment or lab space can I use?
- What's the budget process? (The default budget is £75. Order through the department; never buy anything yourself.)
- Which GenAI category applies?
- Would you look at a draft of my report, and by when?

Write the answers in your logbook straight after the meeting.

### Step 3.2: Write your aim
The aim is **one sentence** that states the overall goal. A useful pattern:

> **To** [verb: quantify / determine / design / evaluate] **[what]**, **by/using** [method], **in order to** [why / who benefits].

> **Weak:** "To look at running shoes and injuries."
>
> **Better:** "To quantify how the cushioning of running-shoe midsoles changes with mileage, using drop-impact tests on new and worn shoes, in order to inform evidence-based shoe replacement."
>
> *(The second one says what will be measured, how, and why it matters.)*

### Step 3.3: Write 4–5 objectives
Objectives are the **specific, checkable steps** that achieve the aim. Make each one **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Start each with an action verb (review, develop, design, build, measure, compare, recommend).

> **Weak:** "Do some testing on shoes."
>
> **Better:** "Measure the peak deceleration of ten shoes at 0, 300 and 600 km of simulated wear using an ASTM F1976-style drop test."

A good set usually runs in this order:
1. review the literature
2. develop a model or method
3. design and build any equipment
4. run the tests
5. analyse the results and make recommendations

**Check your set:** if you met every objective, would the aim be achieved? If not, something is missing.

### Step 3.4: Write your working title
Make it specific: the topic plus what you will actually do. Your supervisor's title ("Sport Injury Biomechanics") can be the main title, with your focus as a subtitle.

**Done when:**
- [ ] Your supervisor has agreed the scope.
- [ ] Your aim is one sentence and passes the "what, how, why" test.
- [ ] You have 4–5 objectives, each starting with a verb and each measurable.

## Stage 4: Research the literature

**Goal:** understand what's already known, so you can show that your project adds something new.

### Step 4.1: Search properly
- **Where to search:**
  - University library search (gives full texts)
  - Google Scholar (good for following citations)
  - the ELE reading list
  - for sports medicine, also PubMed
- **Keywords:** list your main terms and their synonyms, then combine them. For example, *soccer OR football* AND *heading* AND *head acceleration*. Separately, try *neck strength*, *ball pressure*, *headform* and *impact*.
- **Follow citation trails:**
  - *backwards:* the reference lists of good papers
  - *forwards:* "Cited by" in Google Scholar, which finds newer work
- **Include recent work.** Look for papers from 2023–2026; markers reward awareness of recent advances.

### Step 4.2: Judge the quality of each source
Use this order of trust:
1. **Peer-reviewed journal papers.** The best source.
2. **Standards and official bodies** (for example the Laws of the Game, FA guidance, ASTM standards).
3. **Reputable reports.**
4. **News and websites.** Only for context, never as evidence.

### Step 4.3: Read efficiently (the three-pass method)
1. **Pass 1 (5 min):** title, abstract, figures, conclusion. Is it relevant? If not, stop.
2. **Pass 2 (20–30 min):** introduction, method and results. What exactly did they do and find?
3. **Pass 3:** only for your 3–5 most important papers. Read everything, including the limitations.

### Step 4.4: Take notes in a literature matrix
Make this table in Word or Excel and fill in one row per paper. It turns into your literature review almost automatically.

| Ref | Authors (year) | What they did | Key result (with numbers) | Limitation | How it affects my project |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | | |
| 2 | | | | | |

The **Limitation** column is where your critical marks come from. Ask yourself:
- Was the sample small?
- Was it only modelled, not tested?
- Is it out of date?
- Is it a correlation rather than cause and effect?
- Did it leave out something important?

### Step 4.5: Save references as you go
Use a reference manager (Zotero is free) or a simple list. Record the authors, year, title, journal, volume(issue), pages and DOI **the moment you read a paper**. Hunting for them later wastes hours.

**How many sources?** About 12–20 for the preliminary report. Both examples cited only 6–9, so going further is an easy way to stand out. Quality matters more than quantity.

**Done when:**
- [ ] Your literature matrix has 12+ rows, every one with a limitation filled in.
- [ ] You can say in two sentences what is already known and what is **not** known (your gap).
- [ ] All your reference details are saved.

## Stage 5: Do the preliminary work

**Goal:** real evidence that you've started, which shows the main project is feasible.

### What counts as preliminary work
- **Analysis or a model.** Your heading model is one.
- **A small experiment.** Your ball-drop test is one.
- **Design concepts.** Sketches plus a simple comparison.
- **Feasibility checks.** Equipment availability, budget, sensor choice.
- **Meetings** with your supervisor and technical staff.

### How to do it well
1. **The model:** run it, then make sure you understand every assumption. Could you explain *why* the ball is modelled as a pressurised membrane, and what each figure shows? Write the key numbers in your logbook.
2. **The experiment:**
   - Plan the method before you start: what you'll change, what you'll measure, how many repeats (at least 5).
   - Record the raw data exactly as measured, and never edit it.
   - Note what could make it inaccurate, for example reading heights from video, or air resistance.
   - Photograph the set-up.
3. **The design concepts:**
   - Sketch 2–3 different ways the rig could work.
   - Compare them in a small table: cost, accuracy, safety, build time.
   - Say which you prefer and why. This shows engineering judgement, not just one idea.

### How to report preliminary work
Use the same four-part pattern for each piece:
1. **Method:** what you did, briefly.
2. **Result:** with numbers, a figure or a table.
3. **Meaning:** what it tells you.
4. **Next:** how it feeds the main project.

> **Weak:** "I did some drop tests and the results are in Figure 2."
>
> **Better:** "Ten worn and ten new shoes were drop-tested at 5 J (method). Peak deceleration was 18% higher in the worn shoes (result, illustrative number). This suggests cushioning loss is large enough to detect with the planned rig (meaning), so the main tests will use the same set-up at three mileages (next)."

**Done when:**
- [ ] You have at least one result with real numbers from the model and one from your experiment.
- [ ] Your rig concept sketch, and a short comparison of options, is done.
- [ ] Raw data and photos are saved in two places (for example OneDrive and GitHub).

## Stage 6: Write each section

**Goal:** a complete first draft. Write in this order: **Preliminary work → Background → Project plan → Introduction.** You can only introduce something well once you know what you're introducing.

**Word budget for 5 pages with figures:** about **1,000–1,100 words** in total. Introduction ≈200, Background ≈400–450, Preliminary work ≈250–300, Plan ≈150–200.

### 6.1 Preliminary work (≈250–300 words + figures)
- Use the four-part pattern from Stage 5 for each piece of work.
- Every figure or table gets a sentence in the text that says **what it shows**, not just "see Figure 1".
- State at least one limitation honestly, for example "contact time from 240 fps video is only accurate to ±4 ms".
- End with how this work shapes the next stage.

### 6.2 Background and literature review (≈400–450 words)
**Organise by theme, not by paper.** Use 3–5 short themed sub-sections, each combining several papers. For example: the injury problem → how severe impacts are measured → what affects impact severity → test methods → the gap.

**Write critically.** Each key study needs three moves:
1. **Describe:** what they found, briefly.
2. **Evaluate:** a strength or limitation.
3. **Relate:** what this means for *your* project.

> **Descriptive (weak):** "Cook et al. [20] tested running shoes and found that they lost shock absorption with mileage."
>
> **Critical (better):** "Cook et al. [20] found that running shoes lost much of their shock absorption within a few hundred miles. However, the shoes were tested in 1985 and midsole foams have changed a lot since, so the result may not apply to modern shoes. A test of current foams would fill this gap."

**Combine sources (synthesis).** Compare studies with each other:
> "Both [a] and [b] report X, whereas [c] found Y. The difference may be because [c] used Z."

**End with the gap.** The last paragraph turns the review into your project:
> "Although several studies have measured X, none has tested Y under Z conditions. This project therefore …"

**Referencing:**
- Use numbered references [1], [2] … in the order they first appear, as in the module template examples.
- Put the number straight after the claim it supports.
- Paraphrase. Avoid direct quotes in engineering writing.

### 6.3 Project plan, methodology and management (≈150–200 words + Gantt + risk table)
Cover each item the module slide asks for. A short labelled paragraph each works well:
- **Methodology:** the stages of the main project in order (design → build → calibrate → test → analyse), with roughly when each happens.
- **Resources:** equipment, lab space, technician support, the £75 budget and how you'll order parts.
- **Deliverables:** what will exist at the end (a rig, a dataset, a validated model, recommendations, the report).
- **Management:**
  - weekly supervisor meetings with written actions
  - your logbook
  - reviewing the Gantt chart weekly
  - backups
  - "decision gates" (dates by which something must work, with a fallback if it doesn't)
- **Health & safety:**
  - main hazards (for you: flying balls, falling masses, workshop tools, soldering)
  - the controls you'll use
  - the **RAMS** (risk assessment + method statement), which must be approved before any lab work
- **Ethics:** for example, no human participants, so no ethics approval needed. Confirm this with your supervisor.
- **Sustainability:** reuse and borrow equipment, low-power electronics, minimal waste, and how the project benefits society (player health).

**Gantt chart rules:**
- Show the work **from Term 2 onwards** (the template asks for this).
- Use real dates.
- Mark milestones (◆), for example "rig validated" and "final report deadline 3 May".
- Show which tasks depend on others.
- Include the Christmas and Easter breaks and a buffer before deadlines.
- Make it readable at the size it's printed.

**Risk table:** columns for *Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation*. Include **project risks** (late parts, rig not working, illness), not just safety hazards. A good mitigation is a specific action ("bench-test the sensor in December; keep a spare"), not "be careful".

### 6.4 Introduction (≈200 words). Write this last.
Use a **funnel**, from broad to narrow:
1. **Context:** why the topic matters, with one or two numbers and citations.
2. **Problem:** what is unknown or unsolved, from the engineering point of view.
3. **This project:** what you'll do and how, in one or two sentences.
4. **Aim:** one sentence (from Stage 3).
5. **Objectives:** a numbered list of 4–5 (from Stage 3).

### 6.5 Cover page
Fill in every field in the template: title, date, name, programme, student number, candidate number, supervisor.

### Writing style for engineering reports
- **Be precise.** Write "≈20 g at 12 m/s", not "quite a large acceleration". Always give units.
- **Tense:** past for work done ("a model was developed"); future for plans ("the rig will be built").
- **Voice:** the third person ("this project", "the rig") is the safe default. Check with your supervisor if you want to use "I".
- **Keep sentences short:** one idea per sentence, 15–25 words.
- **Avoid vague words:** "very", "a lot", "obviously", "it is clear that".
- **Define abbreviations** the first time: "coefficient of restitution (COR)".

**Done when:**
- [ ] All four sections are drafted, within about 10% of the word budgets.
- [ ] Every claim from a source has a citation.
- [ ] Every figure and table is mentioned in the text.

## Stage 7: Figures, tables and references

**Goal:** professional presentation. Markers notice this immediately.

### Figures and tables
- Number them in order: Figure 1, 2, 3 … and Table 1, 2, 3 …
- Put **figure captions below** the figure and **table captions above** the table.
- Each caption should make sense on its own, for example "Figure 2: Coefficient of restitution against inflation pressure (mean ± SD, 5 drops)".
- Label axes with **units**, and make sure the text is readable when printed.
- Use the same style for all your graphs (same fonts and colours).
- If a figure comes from someone else, cite it in the caption.
- Make tables in Word, not as screenshots. Cut any column or row that doesn't add information.
- Compress photos (Word: File > Compress Pictures) to keep the PDF under 15 MB.

### References
- Use one consistent style. Follow the examples in the module template:
  - **Journal:** Authors (year) "Title", *Journal*, volume(issue), pages.
  - **Web page:** Author/organisation, title, URL, last accessed date.
- Check every entry against the paper itself: names, year, volume and pages.
- Every reference in the list is cited in the text, and every citation in the text is in the list.
- Check you haven't used the same number twice. Example 1 did.

**Done when:**
- [ ] All figures and tables are numbered, captioned and referred to.
- [ ] The reference list is complete, consistent and checked.

## Stage 8: Edit and check

**Goal:** turn a draft into a good report. Do several **separate passes**, each looking for one thing:

1. **Structure pass:** does each section do its job (use the Stage 1 table)? Does the literature review lead to the gap, and the gap to your aim?
2. **Argument pass:** is every claim backed by evidence or a citation? Is each key study evaluated, not just described?
3. **Numbers pass:** are there units everywhere? Do the numbers in the text match the figures and tables?
4. **Concision pass:** cut words, not content. Delete repetition, filler ("it is important to note that") and padding. This is how you meet the page limit. **Never shrink the font or margins.**
5. **Proofreading pass:**
   - set spell-check to English (UK)
   - read the whole report **aloud**
   - check names and technical terms
6. **Format pass:**
   - the cover page is complete
   - headings match the template
   - page numbers are present
   - **5 pages or fewer in the PDF**

**Get feedback:**
- Send your draft to your supervisor if they agreed to read it, and give them a few days.
- Ask a friend to read the introduction: can they explain your aim back to you?

### Self-check: what a strong preliminary report shows
| Section | A strong report… | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Makes the problem clear and gives a precise aim with measurable objectives | Could a stranger say what I'm doing and why after one read? |
| Literature review | Uses a range of good sources *critically* and ends with a clear gap | Does each key paper have a limitation? Is my novel contribution obvious? |
| Preliminary work | Shows real results with numbers and explains what they mean | Is there at least one figure of *my own* results? |
| Plan | Has a credible, dated Gantt chart, specific risks and mitigations, H&S, sustainability and management procedures | Would my supervisor believe this plan? Is any part of the module slide missing? |
| Presentation | Is tidy, consistent, referenced and within 5 pages | Would I be happy for an employer to read this? |

**Done when:**
- [ ] All six passes are complete.
- [ ] The self-check table gets "yes" for every row.

## Appendix: useful phrases

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Context | "X is a growing concern because …" · "Recent evidence suggests that …" |
| Citing | "Smith et al. [3] found that …" · "… has been reported [4, 5]." |
| Comparing | "Similarly, …" · "In contrast, [6] found …" · "These findings agree with …" |
| Evaluating | "However, this study only …" · "A limitation of this approach is …" · "This was not tested under …" |
| The gap | "No study has yet …" · "It remains unclear whether …" · "This project therefore aims to …" |
| Method | "X was measured using …" · "Each test was repeated five times to …" |
| Results | "Figure 2 shows that …" · "X increased by 20% when …" · "This suggests that …" |
| Planning | "The next stage will …" · "If X is not achieved by [date], the fallback is …" |
